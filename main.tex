\title{Pydemi: A Python Package for Interpretable Charge-Density Descriptors \rev{for} Materials Property Prediction} 

\author[1]{Shubham K Maurya}
\author[1]{MD Faiz Akhtar}
\author[1]{Somnath Bhowmick\corref{cor1}}
\ead{bsomnath@iitk.ac.in}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author}
\affiliation[1]{Department of Materials Science and Engineering, Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur, UP 208016, India}

\begin{abstract}
The electronic charge density from a density-functional theory (DFT) calculation describes how a material is bonded, yet it is rarely used as the input of machine-learning models of material properties. Learned density representations predict properties well but are hard to interpret, while interpretable density analysis treats one structure at a time. We present pydemi, an open-source Python package that turns a VASP \texttt{CHGCAR} file into \rev{54} interpretable descriptors of bonding, structure, and site heterogeneity. \rev{Most descriptors change by less than 1\% with the grid or the derivative method, and each} is documented with its formula, units, and range. From the crystal structure alone, pydemi can also predict the density with an included ChargE3Net model; \rev{42 of the 54} descriptors of the predicted densities rank materials almost exactly as their DFT values do. pydemi computed the descriptors of 6,059 inorganic crystals without a failure, in seconds per structure. Gradient-boosted models on the descriptors alone predict 17 elastic, thermal, energetic, electronic and optical properties, mostly with a test $R^2$ between 0.85 and 0.95. SHAP values show which descriptors drive each prediction, and ten descriptors per property are almost as good as all of them. On DFT-relaxed structures unseen by either model, properties predicted from ML-predicted densities are nearly as accurate as those from DFT densities, so \rev{no DFT calculation} is needed. It runs from Python, the command line, or a graphical interface.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
The electronic charge density $\rho(\mathbf{r})$ is the central quantity of
density-functional theory (DFT): it determines the ground state and all of its
properties~\cite{hohenberg1964}. It is also the most direct picture of chemical
bonding that a calculation gives. It shows where electrons accumulate between
atoms, how much charge passes from one atom to another and how localized the
electrons are. The density can also be measured by X-ray diffraction, so the
analysis methods developed for calculated densities are applied to experimental
ones as well~\cite{bader1990,abramov1997,tsirelson2002}. Every plane-wave DFT
calculation produces the density on a real-space grid, and the Materials
Project distributes such densities for a large and continuously updated set of
materials~\cite{jain2013,shen2022}. Large collections of charge densities
therefore already exist, and more are computed every day.

Over the past decade, machine learning (ML) has become a standard tool for
predicting materials properties from such databases~\cite{butler2018,schmidt2019};
we call these models property models.
How accurate a model is, and what can be learned from it, depends largely on how
each material is represented, that is, on its descriptors~\cite{ghiringhelli2015}.
Compositional featurizers compute statistics of elemental
properties~\cite{ward2016,ward2018}. Structural representations encode the
positions of the atoms, either as fixed vectors such as the Coulomb
matrix~\cite{rupp2012} and the smooth overlap of atomic
positions~\cite{bartok2013}, or as graphs from which neural networks learn their
own features~\cite{xie2018,chen2019,choudhary2021,wen2024}. All of these start
from the atoms, not from the electrons. The bonding that the density records is
learned only indirectly, from the composition and the geometry, and the most
accurate models are often the hardest to read. This matters because a materials scientist usually wants to know why
a property takes its value, not only what the value is. When each input of a model
has a physical meaning, the inputs that the model relies on can be compared with
known physics, and they can suggest which feature of the bonding causes a
trend~\cite{rudin2019}. Methods such as SHAP
values~\rev{\cite{lundberg2017}} explain the predictions of any model, but an
explanation in terms of learned features or atom embeddings is still hard to
relate to physics.

Where the density has been used as a model input, it has mostly been as a
learned representation. Three-dimensional convolutional networks read the
density on a voxel grid~\cite{kajita2017,zhao2020} or its compressed latent
representation~\cite{kumar2026}, and persistent homology reduces it to Betti
curves over density thresholds~\cite{szymanski2025}. These representations
predict properties well, which shows that the density carries the information
a property model needs. Their features, however, \rev{describe the density as a
whole and do not correspond one by one to physical quantities}. Such models therefore share the difficulty of the graph networks: they
show how well a property can be predicted, but not which feature of the bonding
sets it. They also have to process the full grid of every structure, which makes
training and prediction costly.

The density has, however, long been analysed without machine learning, and
these analyses give quantities with a clear physical meaning. The quantum theory
of atoms in molecules divides the density into one region per atom, which gives
atomic charges and volumes, and describes each bond by the density at the point
between the two atoms where it is lowest along the bond, which measures the
strength and type of the bond~\cite{bader1990}. Hirshfeld partitioning assigns
the density at each point to the atoms in proportion to how much each free atom
would contribute there~\cite{hirshfeld1977}.
The electron localization function~\cite{becke1990} shows where electrons are
paired, and the non-covalent interaction index~\cite{johnson2010} shows where
weak interactions occur. Each of these quantities has a clear meaning, and
together they describe ionic, covalent and metallic bonding in terms that
chemists and physicists share. They are computed by programs such as
critic2~\cite{oterodelaroza2014} and the Bader code~\cite{henkelman2006}, which
are built to analyse one structure in depth. Their output is a list whose length
varies from structure to structure, with one entry per atom, bond or critical
point, and not a fixed-length vector that a property model can take. The
numerical choices behind the output, such as the grid, the interpolation and the
integration method, are left to the user. Applying these programs to thousands
of structures, in a way that gives the same answer for the same material, is
not what they were designed for.

Density analysis therefore gives interpretable quantities, but not in a form
that a property model can use. A property model needs the same set of numbers
for every material, computed in the same way, for thousands of materials and
ideally also for materials without a DFT calculation, and it has to be shown
that these numbers carry information about the properties. To our knowledge, no
existing tool provides \rev{interpretable density descriptors in this form}, and pydemi was written to do so.

Pydemi is a Python package that turns a VASP density into \rev{54} descriptors.
They describe how the charge is distributed around each nucleus and between the
atoms, the shape of the density over the whole cell, and how much the atoms of a
crystal differ from one another. The package addresses four points. (i) It needs
one input file. Pydemi reads only the \texttt{CHGCAR}, the one density file that
every VASP run writes, so any existing set of calculations can be used without
running them again. Quantities that need the wavefunctions, such as the electron
localization function, are therefore left out, since the density alone gives
them only approximately~\cite{abramov1997,tsirelson2002}. (ii) It gives the same
values for the same material. The grid handling, the derivatives and the
division of the cell into atomic regions are fixed in the package and tested, so
the values \rev{are the same for a cell, its supercell and a shifted or rotated copy} and, for most
descriptors, change by less than 1\% when the grid is made coarser
(Sections~\ref{sec:design} and~\ref{sec:descriptors}). (iii) It does not need a
DFT calculation. Several models predict the density from the \rev{atomic}
structure~\cite{chandrasekaran2019,grisafi2019,jorgensen2022,koker2024}; pydemi
includes one of them, ChargE3Net~\cite{koker2024}, and we measure which
descriptors stay accurate on predicted densities, since descriptors built from
derivatives of the density enlarge small errors in it (Section~\ref{sec:ml}).
(iv) It shows which descriptors matter. We train property models on the
descriptors alone for 17 properties of 6,059 DFT calculations, compare them with
earlier models for the same structures, and identify the descriptors that carry
the information (Section~\ref{sec:properties}). The package can be used as a
Python library, a command-line tool or a graphical interface that runs locally
(Section~\ref{sec:software}), and the code, the tests and the scripts that train
the property models are released under the MIT license.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:design}

Pydemi needs one file, the VASP \texttt{CHGCAR} (or \texttt{CHG}), plain or
compressed. The structure is read from its header and the total density from
the first data block, which is divided by the cell volume to give $\rho$ in
e\,\AA$^{-3}$. Spin-polarized and non-collinear files are accepted, and their
magnetization blocks are ignored. No other VASP file (\texttt{POTCAR},
\texttt{OUTCAR}, \texttt{AECCAR}, \texttt{ELFCAR}, \texttt{LOCPOT}) is needed,
and the \rev{covalent radii used for the core, bond and interstitial regions} are tabulated in the
package. Because every VASP run writes a \texttt{CHGCAR}, pydemi can be applied to
existing calculations without running VASP again, and every descriptor can be
computed for every structure of a dataset. The density can also come from a crystal structure through the
included ChargE3Net model (Section~\ref{sec:ml}). Both routes give the same
density object (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}), an instance of the
\texttt{VolumetricData} class of the package, which holds the structure, the density grid and a note of where the density came from; this note
is written into the metadata of every output row.

From the density grid, pydemi first computes the fields and the geometry that
the descriptors need, and it computes each of them only once per structure. A
derived quantity, such as the gradient, the Hartree potential or the map from
each grid point to its nearest atom, is stored on the density object
the first time a descriptor asks for it, and every later descriptor that
needs it reuses the stored copy. The settings it was computed with (for example
the derivative method or the \rev{region radii}) are stored with it, so a structure
processed again with other settings gets new fields and never stale ones.

Several descriptors need the gradient and the Laplacian of the density, and
two need the Hartree potential of the electrons. The density is stored on a grid
that runs along the three cell vectors, so pydemi first takes derivatives along
these grid directions, with respect to the fractional coordinates $u_a$, and
then converts them to Cartesian ones. When the cell vectors are not at right
angles, as in hexagonal, monoclinic and triclinic cells, a step along one grid
direction is partly a step along another, and the Laplacian then also contains
mixed derivatives between pairs of grid directions:
\[
\nabla^2 f=\sum_{a,b}G_{ab}\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial u_a\,\partial u_b},
\]
where $G=BB^{\mathsf T}$ and the rows of $B$ are the reciprocal lattice vectors.
The mixed terms ($a\neq b$) are zero only when the cell vectors are at right
angles. They are needed: for kyanite (Al$_2$SiO$_5$, triclinic) in our dataset,
leaving them out changes the Laplacian by up to 15\% of its largest value and
the negative-Laplacian volume fraction from 0.060 to 0.072. With them, pydemi
reproduces the exact Laplacian of a test density with a known analytic form to
round-off. By default the derivatives are computed from the Fourier transform of
the density. VASP writes the density as a finite sum of plane waves, and the
derivative of each plane wave is known exactly, so this method has no error from
the grid spacing beyond computer round-off. Finite differences, which estimate
derivatives from neighbouring grid points, are available as an alternative and
are compared in Section~\ref{sec:descriptors}. The Hartree potential is obtained
from the same Fourier transform by solving Poisson's equation. For a periodic
crystal its average value is not defined, so pydemi measures the potential from
its average over the cell.

Many descriptors need to know, for every grid point, which nucleus is
nearest, how far away it is and in which direction. Pydemi finds these with a
$k$-d tree, a standard data structure that finds the nearest of many points
quickly, built over the atoms and their periodic images. In strongly sheared
cells the nearest image can lie beyond the usual $3\times3\times3$ block of
cells, so the search is widened until it is guaranteed to contain it. Some grid
points lie at exactly the same distance from two or more nuclei, which is common
when atoms sit on grid points, as in rock-salt MgO. Such a point has to be given
to one of these nuclei. A simple choice, the nucleus listed first in the file,
would make the result depend on how the file is written: the same crystal with
its atoms listed in another order, or written as a supercell, could give the
point to a different nucleus, and the descriptors would change slightly although
the material is the same. Pydemi instead chooses the nucleus from its position
relative to the point, which depends only on where the atoms are, so a cell, its
supercell and a translated or rotated copy give the same descriptors
(Section~\ref{sec:descriptors}). \rev{The covalent radii $R_i$~\cite{cordero2008}
also divide the cell into three regions. Around every nucleus $i$ lie a core
sphere of radius $aR_i$ and an outer sphere of radius $bR_i$. A grid point
belongs to the core if it lies inside any core sphere, to the bond region if
it lies inside an outer sphere but in no core sphere, and to the interstitial
region if it lies outside every outer sphere. The factors $a=0.79$ and $b=1.14$
are the 1st and 99th percentiles of $d/(R_A+R_B)$, the distance of an atom to
its nearest neighbour divided by the sum of their covalent radii, over all
atoms of our dataset. The core spheres of two bonded atoms therefore almost
never overlap, and almost no bond passes through the interstitial region. The
regions scale with the size of the atoms and need no fixed length;
\texttt{pydemi sweep} shows how the descriptors change with $a$ and $b$
(Section~\ref{sec:software}).} The 18 site descriptors, which are computed for each atom, need the cell to be
divided into atomic regions. \rev{For the bond-region charge of each atom, the
spheres decide: a point of the bond region belongs to the atom with the
smallest $r/R_i$, so that the bond regions of the atoms add up to that of the
cell. For $m_1$ and $\zeta$, a}ll results in this work use the default, which
gives each grid point to its nearest nucleus. \rev{The} fuzzy cells of
Becke~\cite{becke1988}, which share the points between neighbouring atoms
smoothly, are offered as \rev{an alternative. The 12 site descriptors of $m_1$
and $\zeta$ are defined for the chosen partition, and} Section~S2 of the Supporting Information
shows how \rev{they change with it}.

Each descriptor is a short Python function that takes the density object and
returns one number. All descriptors are kept in a single list, the registry,
together with a record of each: its name, domain, units, range of values and
references, the cached quantities it needs, and the cases in which
it is undefined, with the value returned instead. Everything else is built from
this list: the columns of the output, which always appear in the same order, the
descriptor catalogue and the invariance tests of Section~\ref{sec:software}.
In this work, the descriptors are computed for the charge densities of 6,059
inorganic crystals of 87 elements from our earlier study~\cite{kumar2026}. The
structures were taken from the Materials Project, and the densities were
computed with VASP~\rev{\cite{kresse1996,kresse1996cms}}, the PBE functional~\cite{perdew1996} and
PAW potentials~\cite{blochl1994,kresse1999}, with a plane-wave cutoff of
500\,eV, a $k$-point spacing of 0.1\,\AA$^{-1}$ and a final calculation with the
tetrahedron method\rev{~\cite{blochl1994tetra}}. The set contains elemental solids, intermetallic compounds
and compounds of metals with non-metals (borides, carbides, hydrides,
pnictides, chalcogenides, oxides and halides), so metallic, covalent and ionic
bonding are all represented. Section~S6 of the Supporting Information gives the
DFT settings in full and the number of structures in each class.

units, and the reference document of the package also gives the ranges and
references. Below, $k$
runs over the grid points and $r_k$ is the distance from grid point $k$ to its

The bonding descriptors ask how the charge is arranged around the atoms and in
the bonds. The radial moments $m_n=\sum_k\rho_k r_k^n/\sum_k\rho_k$ and the \rev{region}
fractions describe how far the charge sits from its nearest nucleus; a large
interstitial fraction points to delocalized charge between the atoms. The
non-radial gradient share $\zeta$ is zero for spherical, non-overlapping atoms
and grows as the charge around the atoms becomes less spherical, for example
when it is drawn into bonds.
The Laplacian descriptors locate where charge is concentrated
($\nabla^2\rho<0$). One of them compares the regions where charge is
concentrated with those where it is depleted ($\nabla^2\rho>0$). It is the
fraction of the total $|\nabla^2\rho|$ that comes from points with
$\nabla^2\rho<0$, and only points outside the atomic cores are counted.
Because the cell is periodic, the two kinds of region cancel exactly over the
whole cell, so without this restriction the fraction would be $\tfrac12$ for
every crystal. The bounded bond ellipticity describes the shape of the charge across
a bond, from 0 when it is round (cylindrical) to close to 1 when it is
flattened, and unlike the usual ellipticity\rev{~\cite{bader1983}} it cannot become infinite.
\rev{With $\lambda_1\le\lambda_2<0$ the two negative curvatures of the density, the usual
ellipticity $\varepsilon=\lambda_1/\lambda_2-1$ diverges where $\lambda_2$ approaches zero;
the bounded form $1-\lambda_2/\lambda_1=\varepsilon/(1+\varepsilon)$ is a one-to-one function
of $\varepsilon$ at each point and differs from it only by compressing large values. It is
averaged over the bond region rather than taken at the bond critical points, so it measures
the anisotropy of the density across the bond region as a whole, not the ellipticity of
individual bonds.} The
non-covalent interaction (NCI) descriptors pick out the low-density,
low-gradient regions of weak interactions~\cite{johnson2010}. The structural
descriptors treat the density of the whole cell. The percolation level along
each cell vector is the highest density at which the region of higher density
still forms an unbroken path through the crystal in that direction; it is high
for metals and close to zero for ionic solids. The other structural
descriptors count the maxima, minima and non-nuclear maxima of the density and
measure its lowest value, its directionality and its information content. The
site heterogeneity descriptors measure how much the atoms of one crystal
differ. Each atom is given $m_1$, $f_{bond}$ and $\zeta$ from its own atomic
region, and their spread, range, maximum and minimum over the atoms are
reported. The variance over the
atoms is also split into two parts that add up to the total. The
within-element part comes from differences between atoms of the same element,
such as the Al atoms of kyanite (Al$_2$SiO$_5$), which sit in four different
surroundings. The between-element part comes from differences between the
average Al, Si and O atoms.

A descriptor should describe the material, not the way it was computed. The
density grid is not set by the material. VASP chooses it from the plane-wave
cutoff, so two calculations of the same compound can give grids of different
fineness. If a descriptor changed noticeably with the grid, the same material
could get different values, and a model trained on the descriptor would learn
this noise instead of the material. We therefore recomputed the descriptors of
structures of our dataset on a coarser grid with about half as many points, as
a calculation with a lower cutoff would give. The median change is below 1\%
for \rev{45 of the 54} descriptors. This is expected, because the density in a
\texttt{CHGCAR} is smooth and most descriptors are averages over the whole
cell, which a coarser grid reproduces almost exactly. Of the nine that change
more, four change by less than 2\%\rev{: the valence Laplacian concentration, the
signed NCI density and the between-element variances of $f_{bond}$ and $\zeta$}. For four others, the percolation anisotropy
and the three within-element variances, the value of the descriptor itself is
close to zero for many materials. In a cubic crystal the density connects
equally well along the three cell vectors, so the percolation anisotropy is
nearly zero, and when all atoms of an element are equivalent by symmetry, their
within-element variance is nearly zero. A very small change in such a value is
a large percentage of it, so these descriptors show large relative changes
although their values hardly change. The last, \rev{the charge of the non-nuclear
maxima, rests on a count of maxima}, and a coarser grid can miss a small maximum.
\rev{Measured in absolute terms, in units of the standard deviation of each
descriptor over the dataset, 46 of the 54 change by less than 0.1 in 95\% of the
structures and keep the order of the structures ($\rho_s\geq0.95$). The other
eight are sensitive to the grid: the counts of density maxima, minima and
non-nuclear maxima and the charge of the non-nuclear maxima, the three
within-element variances, whose values are near zero for most structures but
whose order changes ($\rho_s=0.82$), and the site range of $f_{bond}$. These
eight should be compared only between densities computed with the same
plane-wave cutoff.}
Section~S2 of the Supporting Information lists the \rev{relative and absolute} change of every descriptor,
and also how much the descriptors change when the derivatives are computed by
finite differences \rev{(with fourth-order differences, all 54 stay within the limits above)} or when the atomic regions are defined differently.

A descriptor must also stay the same when the crystal is described
differently. The same material can be written down in many ways, and a
descriptor must not depend on the choice. We tested this on the density of a
low-symmetry crystal from our dataset, triclinic CuAuF$_5$. Every descriptor
was computed four times, for the original cell, for a cell eight times larger,
for the cell with its origin moved by a whole number of grid steps, and for the
cell rotated in space. All four gave the same value for all \rev{54} descriptors.
\rev{With the cell vectors relabelled, 52 of the 54 were unchanged. Only the
percolation levels depend on the choice of cell vectors, because each is defined
along one of them: exchanging two vectors exchanges their values, while the
percolation anisotropy stays the same.}

\section{Descriptors from a predicted density}
\label{sec:ml}

pydemi includes ChargE3Net~\cite{koker2024}, a neural network that predicts the
electron density of a crystal from its atomic structure alone. Its authors
released the network together with weights trained on the charge densities of
the Materials Project~\cite{shen2022}. \rev{A predicted density can be compared
with a DFT density, or used in its place, only if both stand for the same
calculation settings, the PAW potentials included. For some elements the
Materials Project used other PAW potentials than our dataset, with a different
number of valence electrons. We therefore} took the released network as the starting point and trained it
further on the densities of our dataset (fine-tuning), with 5,152 structures for
training, 302 for validation and 605 for testing. This gave a lower error than
the same network trained from scratch on our data alone (Section~S2 of the
Supporting Information). The fine-tuned model is included in pydemi and covers
87 elements, every element from hydrogen to plutonium except Kr, Xe, Po, At, Rn,
Fr and Ra.

Given only a crystal structure, pydemi predicts the density on the grid that
VASP itself would choose for that cell with a 500\,eV energy cutoff, and scales
it so that it holds exactly the valence electrons of the atoms in the cell. The
result has the same form as a \texttt{CHGCAR} written by a DFT calculation, so
all \rev{54} descriptors are computed from it in the same way, with no change to the
analysis. Its cost grows with the number of atoms and grid points, and a GPU is
used when one is available. Technical details are given in Section~S2 of the Supporting Information.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_ml.pdf}
\caption{Descriptors from predicted densities: the descriptors of the test

\label{fig:ml}
\end{figure}

The predicted densities are close to the DFT ones. For half of the 605
test structures, the difference between the predicted and the DFT density, taken
point by point and added up over the whole grid, is below 0.6\% of the total
charge. It
is somewhat larger for crystals with lanthanides or actinides. The question for
pydemi is whether such small differences change the descriptors. We
therefore computed each descriptor from both densities for the 605 test
materials and compared the two sets of values in two ways. First, we checked
whether the descriptor sorts the materials in the same order from both
densities, using the Spearman rank correlation $\rho_s$, which is 1 when the two
orders are identical and becomes smaller as more materials change places. This
matters because a property model learns how a property changes from one material
to another, and a descriptor that mixes up the order of the materials would
mislead it. Second, we measured how much the values themselves differ (median
relative difference, Fig.~\ref{fig:ml}b). For \rev{42 of the 54} descriptors, $\rho_s$
is at least 0.95, and for 37 of them at least 0.99, and most of them change by
less than 1\%. These \rev{42} are all 18 heterogeneity descriptors, \rev{15} of the 18
bonding descriptors and \rev{9} of the \rev{18} structural descriptors (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml}a). The other \rev{12} fall into four groups, each with a
clear reason. The counts of maxima and minima of the density change because the
prediction adds or removes tiny bumps in flat regions far from the atoms,
without changing the density noticeably. The number and charge of non-nuclear
maxima are zero in both densities for most structures, so their ranking is
decided by a few materials. The bounded bond ellipticity\rev{, the valence Laplacian concentration} and the volume
fraction of negative Laplacian use second derivatives of the density in
low-density regions, and derivatives magnify the small errors of the
prediction. The last group are five anisotropy measures, namely the three eigenvalues and the
fractional anisotropy of the tensor of gradient directions, and the percolation
anisotropy. They are predicted accurately, but many materials share almost the
same value (every cubic crystal, for instance, is isotropic), so tiny
differences reorder them. Several of these values are close to zero, so their
relative error in Fig.~\ref{fig:ml}b looks large although the absolute
difference is small. When
the density is predicted, the \rev{42} can therefore be used as they are, and the
other \rev{12} with care. For these \rev{12}, the value from a predicted density
should not be used to rank or compare individual materials; where a DFT
density exists, its value should be used instead.

\section{Property prediction}
\label{sec:properties}

\subsection{Data and protocol}
We used the descriptors to predict 17 properties of the materials of the dataset,
all downloaded from the Materials Project~\cite{jain2013}
(database version 2026.04.13). They are the bulk, shear and Young's moduli; the
elastic constants $C_{11}$, $C_{12}$ and $C_{44}$~\cite{dejong2015}; the Debye
temperature, the two sound velocities and the minimum thermal conductivities
in the Clarke and Cahill models~\cite{clarke2003,cahill1992}; the formation and
cohesive energies; the PBE band gap, whether a material is a metal and whether
its gap is direct; and the refractive index\rev{, $n=\sqrt{\varepsilon_\infty}$ from the
electronic dielectric constant computed with density-functional perturbation
theory}~\cite{petousis2017}. We used
the random split that comes with the dataset~\cite{kumar2026}: 4,847 structures
for training, 606 for validation and 606 for testing. A material without a
value for a property is left out for that property only.

For each property we trained three kinds of models on the \rev{54} descriptors: a linear model, a random forest~\rev{\cite{breiman2001,pedregosa2011}}
and gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM~\cite{ke2017}). The settings of LightGBM
were tuned automatically on the validation set~\cite{akiba2019}, and the test
set was used only once, for the final scores. Each random forest and LightGBM
model was trained five times with different random seeds, and we report the average. To find out which
descriptors the models rely on, we used SHAP values~\cite{lundberg2020}, which
split each prediction into the contributions of the individual descriptors. We
also retrained every model on only the ten most important descriptors of its
property, chosen on the training set. All details are given in
Section~S3 of the Supporting Information. LightGBM was the most accurate of the
three models for 16 properties and as accurate as the random forest for the
direct gap (Supporting Information, Table~S3). Its test scores are
given in Table~\ref{tab:properties} and Fig.~\ref{fig:properties}.

\subsection{Results and interpretation}
With the descriptors alone, LightGBM predicts every regression property
except the refractive index with a test $R^2$ between \rev{0.86} and 0.95
(Table~\ref{tab:properties}, Fig.~\ref{fig:properties}). The refractive index,
which has the fewest materials, reaches 0.76. Metallicity is classified with a
ROC-AUC of \rev{0.99} \rev{(73\% of the 605 test materials are metals)}. \rev{The direct
gap, defined only for materials with a gap,} is classified with \rev{a ROC-AUC of}
0.79 \rev{(48 of the 163 test materials have a direct gap)}.

To place these scores, we compared the descriptors with the models of Kumar et~al.~\cite{kumar2026}, which were trained on the same structures with the same
split (Table~\ref{tab:compare}). These models use a compressed representation of the full density grid,
compositional (Magpie) features, both together, and the graph neural network MEGNet~\cite{chen2019}. We also trained MatTen,
an equivariant graph neural network for elastic properties~\cite{wen2024}, on
the same data (Section~S3 of the Supporting Information). For this comparison,
our models were trained on the target values of Kumar et~al.~\cite{kumar2026}. For the
three moduli and the Debye temperature these are the Materials Project values of
Table~\ref{tab:properties}, with the Debye temperature given for fewer
materials. The energy of Kumar et~al. is computed relative to the free
atoms from the DFT energies of the dataset, so it differs from the cohesive
energy of Table~\ref{tab:properties}, and our models were trained on it
separately. For the three moduli, the descriptors alone give the highest $R^2$
of all models. For the Debye temperature they are above the density and
composition models and level with MEGNet and MatTen. For
the energy relative to the free atoms they are well above the density
representation alone \rev{and above the attention networks, but below MEGNet,
MatTen and LightGBM on the density representation and the composition together}. This energy depends strongly on which elements are present, which
the composition describes directly.

\rev{The volume fraction of the NCI region is the most important descriptor
overall: it leads for the band gap, metallicity, the formation energy and the
refractive index. The elastic moduli and constants rely mainly on the mean
interstitial, bond-midpoint and percolation densities, the} thermal properties \rev{on the
charge-distance variance and the valence Laplacian concentration, and the
cohesive energy on the interstitial charge fraction and the bond-midpoint density}; only the direct-gap classification has no dominant
descriptor (Supporting Information, Fig.~S5). The information is concentrated
in a few descriptors. Models trained on only the ten most important descriptors
of each property, chosen on the training set (Fig.~\ref{fig:top10};
Supporting Information, Table~S4), are almost as accurate as those trained on
all \rev{54}. Their test score is lower by only 0.01 to \rev{0.08}
(Table~\ref{tab:properties}). Removing the \rev{16} descriptors that correlate
strongly with another one ($|\rho_s|\geq0.90$ on the training set) changes the
test score by at most \rev{0.012 for every property} (Supporting Information, Tables~S5 and S6).
Across all properties, these lists together use \rev{32 of the 54} descriptors, and
a few of them appear for most properties, led by \rev{the interstitial charge
fraction and the NCI volume fraction (13 of the 17 lists each) and the mean
interstitial density} (Fig.~\ref{fig:top10}). For most
properties, eight or nine of these ten descriptors are among the \rev{42} that a
predicted density reproduces well (Section~\ref{sec:ml}). The property models
should therefore work almost as well with a predicted density, and the next
paragraph tests this.

The descriptors can also be computed from a predicted density, and we tested
whether they still serve the property models. We trained the property models
again with the same protocol, leaving out the 605 test structures of the
density model, and evaluated them on these structures, which neither the
density model nor the property models had seen, once with the descriptors of the DFT
density and once with those of the ChargE3Net density
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mlprops}). This answers two questions: whether the two routes
give the same prediction, and how close each prediction is to the DFT value.
Because these models were trained on fewer materials (4,365 instead of 4,847)
and tested on other structures, their scores differ from those of
Table~\ref{tab:properties}, most for the shear-related properties and the
refractive index.

The two routes give nearly the same predictions. For every regression property
the $R^2$ between them is at least \rev{0.95, except for the formation energy
and the refractive index (0.91)}, and they place \rev{97.2\%} of the
structures in the same metal or non-metal class. Accuracy is almost unchanged
for most properties. The bulk modulus has a test $R^2$ of \rev{0.96 from the DFT
density and 0.95 from the predicted one}, and most other properties lose
\rev{less than 0.03}. The losses are largest for \rev{the formation energy (0.07)
and the band gap (0.04). Among the regression models, the formation energy
relies most on the 12 descriptors that a predicted density reproduces less well
(Section~\ref{sec:ml}), together with the refractive index: the 12 carry 26\% of
the importance in its model, against 12\% in the bulk-modulus model, and the
bounded bond ellipticity alone causes 36\% of the difference between the two
routes.} Two properties are weak by both routes. Whether a gap is direct is
predicted with an ROC-AUC of \rev{0.75 from both densities}. For the refractive
index, the $R^2$ is \rev{0.79} from the DFT density and \rev{0.58} from
the predicted one for 128 of the 131 test materials. The other three,
InAgTe$_2$, LiVRh$_2$ and NaNiN, have DFT refractive indices of 14 to 24, far
above the 1.4 to 5.5 of the rest. Such large values are rare in the training
data (12 of 990 materials), and both routes predict values between \rev{3 and 7} for
these three. With them included, the $R^2$ drops to \rev{0.23 by both routes}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mlprops}). All values are given in
Section~S4 and Table~S7 of the Supporting Information.

The test structures were relaxed with DFT. Given such a structure, pydemi
obtains the descriptors and the properties without \rev{a DFT calculation of} the density.
Unlike graph networks, which predict a property directly from the
structure, this route passes through the same \rev{54} descriptors as for a DFT
density, each with a physical meaning. A user can therefore see which feature of
the bonding drives a prediction, compare the material with those of the dataset,
and check the descriptors against a DFT density once one is computed.

\section{Software}
\label{sec:software}

pydemi is written in Python and is installed with
\texttt{pip install pydemi}. This core installation reads \texttt{CHGCAR}
files, computes the \rev{54} descriptors of one structure or of many, and gives their
catalogue, from Python and from the command line. It is built on
NumPy~\cite{harris2020}, SciPy~\cite{virtanen2020} and
pandas~\cite{mckinney2010}. \texttt{CHGCAR} is the density format of VASP; a
density from another code, such as Quantum ESPRESSO, can be read once
it is written in this format, but the density and property models were trained
on VASP densities. Three optional extras add the graphical
interface (\texttt{pydemi[gui]}\rev{, built on Streamlit~\cite{streamlit} and
Plotly~\cite{plotly}}), the density
model that predicts a density from a crystal structure
(\texttt{pydemi[ml]}, built on PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019}, e3nn~\cite{geiger2022} and
ASE~\cite{larsen2017}) and the property models (\texttt{pydemi[props]},
with LightGBM~\cite{ke2017}). A descriptor reference with the definition, formula,
physical meaning and limits of every descriptor is included and is opened from
the interface.

In Python, a density is read with \texttt{read\_chgcar} and its descriptors are
computed with \texttt{featurize}, or with \texttt{featurize\_batch} for many
files. \texttt{catalogue} returns the name, units, range and formula of every
descriptor, \texttt{ml.predict} predicts a density from a crystal structure, and
\texttt{props.predict} predicts properties from the descriptors. The command
line offers the same steps (\texttt{pydemi featurize}, \texttt{batch},
\texttt{catalogue}, \texttt{predict} and \texttt{gui}), and \texttt{pydemi
sweep} shows how the descriptors change with the \rev{sphere factors $a$ and $b$}. Batch runs
write one row per structure, with the descriptors and the run metadata; they continue past structures that fail
and record the error.

On our dataset, a batch run computed all descriptors of every structure without
a failure. On our computer (two Intel Xeon Gold 6338N processors), the
descriptors of one structure took 3.3\,s in the median, and those of the whole
dataset took 37 minutes with 16 structures computed in parallel. The
time depends mainly on the size of the density grid, not on the number of
atoms. Even the largest \texttt{CHGCAR} of the dataset (350\,MB) took 54\,s
and \rev{at most 2.8}\,GB of memory (Section~S6 of the
Supporting Information).

The graphical interface (\texttt{pydemi gui}) starts a local server and opens in
the browser (Fig.~\ref{fig:gui}). An input panel on the left opens a \texttt{CHGCAR}, or a crystal structure file (CIF, POSCAR or any format ASE reads) whose density is then
predicted in the background, with its progress shown and the option to cancel.
Below it are a summary of the structure and the grid and the choice of how the cell is divided between the atoms
(Section~\ref{sec:design}). The main area has five views. The structure view shows the cell with
density slices through any lattice plane. The descriptor view lists the
descriptors by the names of Tables~\ref{tab:families} and~\ref{tab:families2}
in a searchable table, each with its formula and a figure of the
quantity it is computed from, and the plot view steps through these figures one
descriptor at a time. The property view gives the predicted properties with the
mean absolute error of each model on the test set, its test-set parity plot, its most important inputs
and, for structures of the dataset, the DFT values. The compare view (with the ML
extra) takes the \texttt{CHGCAR} shown in the input panel, predicts the density of the same structure on the same
grid, and shows the density error and the descriptors and properties from both
densities side by side. All results can be exported.

The package is checked by automated tests. The descriptors are tested on
model densities built from simple functions, Gaussian and Slater-type atoms,
for which the exact value of each descriptor is known from a formula, and
against rules that hold for any density, for example that the charge fractions
of the core, bond and interstitial regions add up to one. The invariance check
of Section~\ref{sec:descriptors} is also run automatically for every
descriptor, on a small model crystal. The reader is tested with \texttt{CHGCAR} files in
every layout VASP writes, including spin-polarized and non-collinear runs and the
older VASP~4 layout. The density model is tested by comparing its prediction for
one structure with the output of the original ChargE3Net code. The property models
are tested by comparing their predictions with those saved when the models were
trained. The interface is tested by checking that each figure is drawn from the
same numbers that give its descriptor. The tests run automatically on Linux, macOS
and Windows each time the code changes, and Section~S5 of the Supporting
Information lists all \rev{346} of them.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
pydemi turns the one file that every VASP calculation writes, the
\texttt{CHGCAR}, into \rev{54} descriptors, each with a short physical meaning and a
value largely set by the material rather than by the grid or the numerical
method. It computed every descriptor for every crystal of our dataset without a
failure. Automated tests compare the descriptors with exact values for model
densities and check that every descriptor stays the same when the cell is
enlarged, moved or rotated. It can be used
as a Python library, from the command line or through a local graphical
interface. The included ChargE3Net model gives the descriptors from a crystal
structure alone. For \rev{42 of the 54}, the predicted density sorts the materials in
the same order as the DFT density ($\rho_s \geq 0.95$). The other \rev{12} are the
counts of density maxima and minima, the non-nuclear maxima, \rev{three} descriptors
built from second derivatives \rev{of the density}, and five anisotropy
measures whose values cluster.

Used alone, the descriptors predict 17 elastic, thermal, energetic,
electronic and optical properties. SHAP values show which descriptors drive
each prediction, and ten descriptors per property are almost as good as all of
them. On the same structures, they predict the three elastic moduli more
accurately than models built on the full density, on composition or on graph
neural networks. On DFT-relaxed structures that neither model had seen, properties predicted from
ML-predicted densities are nearly as accurate as those from DFT densities, and
the small losses come from the descriptors that transfer less well. pydemi can
therefore predict the properties of a relaxed crystal structure without
\rev{a DFT calculation of} its density.
